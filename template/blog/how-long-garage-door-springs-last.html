The honest answer to "how long do garage door springs last" is that nobody rates them in years. Springs are rated in cycles, and how many years that works out to depends entirely on how often the door goes up and down. Here is how the math works, what the options are, and how to tell when the springs on your {{city}} door are getting near the end.

What a cycle is

One cycle is one full open and one full close. Opening the door to pull the car out in the morning and closing it behind you is a single cycle. If the garage is the family's main entrance, the dog goes in and out through it, and the kids use it as the front door, a household can easily run 6 to 8 cycles a day without thinking about it.

The standard torsion spring fitted on most residential doors is rated for about 10,000 cycles. That is the industry baseline, not a premium figure. Here is what it means in practice:

  • 2 cycles a day (a garage used mostly for storage): roughly 13 to 14 years.
  • 4 cycles a day (one car, normal commuting): about 7 years.
  • 6 to 8 cycles a day (two cars, garage is the main entrance): 3 to 5 years.

When homeowners tell us a spring "only lasted five years," it has usually done exactly what it was rated for. The spring did not fail early; the door simply gets more use than people realize.

High-cycle springs

A high-cycle spring is a longer spring with a thicker wire that produces the same lifting force but flexes less on each cycle, so it fatigues more slowly. Common ratings are 25,000 cycles and 50,000 cycles. On a busy two-car door, a 25,000-cycle spring takes the replacement interval from about four years to ten or more.

The cost difference is modest. The springs cost somewhat more, but the labor to install them is the same, and labor is most of the bill. For a door used as the main entrance we almost always recommend the upgrade, because the standard-spring replacements you avoid cost far more than the difference. The only time it is not an option is when the shaft between the drums is too short for the longer coil, which is rare on modern doors.

Single doors and two-car doors

A single 8- or 9-foot door is usually light enough to run on one torsion spring. A 16-foot two-car door almost always needs two, one on each side of the center bearing plate. Two springs share the load, which is why a two-car door with one broken spring will sometimes still lift a few inches before the opener gives up: the surviving spring is doing half the job on its own.

The spring count also matters when it comes time to replace. On a two-spring door, both springs were installed on the same day, have done the same number of cycles, and are the same distance from failure. Replacing only the one that broke saves a small amount today and nearly guarantees a second service call within months. Signs the springs are wearing out

Springs do not usually go from fine to broken with no warning. A few things to watch for:

  • The door feels heavy. Pull the red release cord with the door closed and lift it by hand. A balanced door lifts with one hand and stays put at waist height. If it takes real effort or drifts down on its own, the springs have lost tension.
  • The opener strains. The motor sounds labored, the door moves slower than it used to, or the opener lifts the door a foot and reverses. The opener is being asked to lift weight the spring should be carrying.
  • Visible wear. Rust, a gap in the coil, or a spring that looks stretched or slightly uneven along its length.
  • Age. If the springs are original on a house built ten or more years ago and the door is used daily, they are living on borrowed time whether or not they show symptoms.

A door that fails the balance test is also hard on the opener. Replacing springs before they break is cheaper than replacing springs and then an opener gear a few months later. If the opener is already struggling, our opener repair service can check it on the same visit.

Getting more life out of the springs you have

You cannot add cycles to a spring, but you can keep it from failing early. Spray the coils with a silicone or lithium garage door lubricant twice a year to slow rust, keep the tracks clean and the rollers rolling so the spring is not fighting friction, and have the balance checked during routine maintenance.

When a spring breaks

The usual sign is a loud bang from the garage, often at night or on a cold morning, followed by a door that will not open more than a few inches. Do not keep pressing the opener button and do not try to lift the door by hand with a helper. A 300-pound door with no counterbalance can come down fast.
